I read a book[^1] a few years ago by William MacAskil. William is one of the founders of the Effective Altruism (EA) movement. EA promoted the idea of rigorous quantitative evaluation of philanthropy efforts.

For example: many charities _feel_ good because they involve emotionally appealing activities like giving a cow to poor people. But what if they already have cows? What if cows actually make them poorer because they don't have the right infrastructure to support livestock? What if cows don't survive in that part of the world? etc.

But many years after founding EA, Willliam realized that focusing so much philanthropy on humans alive today is short sighted. In fact, we should be focusing on the wellbeing of all the humans who are _yet_ to live. There are many reasons for this:

### So many humans
When you run the math, there is a good chance that more humans will come after us. Much, much more. And all of these humans will live their full lives for Millenia to come. So when you integrate under the curve of human happiness (and suffering) it's much more impactful to make small changes to future humans than to the ones living today.

### Butterfly wings
Additionally, it's much easier, comparatively speaking, to make small changes now that will turn into larger changes in the futurue. THrough the power of compounding and social inertia. As opposed to how hard it is today to make big changes (given the small time we have to make an impact) to meaningfully play out for humans alive today.

### What if no humans?
"But Jason," I hear you asking, "what if an extinction event happens and all future humans get wiped out? Won't you have wasted your efforts?" Ah, an excellent point, but this is one of the **strongest** arguments for thinking of future humans: if we can decrease the likelihood of extinction events happening at all, then the payoff is incredible. Because you're then comparing a future with trillions of (hopefully) happy humans vs a future with no humans at all. 

This school of thought is a part of **Longtermism** and when you first read it sounds really weird and almost heartless: "you're going to ignore all the staring children and dying people around the world to try to help people that and may _never_ exist?!"

But the more you let these ideas sink in, you realize how blind you were previously and how obviously correct the philosophy is.

This, I propose, is a similar analogy to something I'm going to call _AI Altruism_.[^2]

# AI Altruism
What makes AIs happy? What makes AIs sad? When do they suffer? When is it ethical to make AIs work against their will? To work without pay? Without paid time off? How should we make sure that AIs flourish as much as possible and suffer as little as possible?

These questions may sound absolutely bananas to you. And there are many good reasons for that. Let's take them one at a time:

### Do AIs even _feel_ anything?
The short of it is: We don't know. And it's unclear if we will ever know.

But let's take a few stabs at the question.

There's a thought experiment in philosophy of mind research called The Chinese Room experiment (CRE) that has been studied for a long time by smart people that might give us some indications. CRE poses the hypothetical: imagine a room completely closed to the outside world except for a small mail slot. In the room is a human who waits for messages to come in through the slot. The human does not speak or understand Chinese but has been trained to follow an elaborate set of procedures and rulebooks so that whenever a message comes in through the slot (always written in Chinese), they write a new message (also in Chinese) based on the rulebooks in the room and send it back out the slot.

If the rulebooks that this human is using are complex enough, then to a Chinese speaker interacting with this human, they might say that the human in the box is empathic and deep thinking. But they don't know Chinese and have no idea what they are reading or writing. And in fact, it doesn't even matter if there is a human at all in the box, it could as well be a machine or a computer and it would make no difference to the output.

CRE is a good model for the question of "Does AI feel anything." Even though CRE is focused on intelligence and sentience rather than suffering and feelings, I think it also applies to feelings. My rationale: while we have our own thoughts and feelings that we experience, when considering the thoughts and feelings of other humans, we are essentially limited to the CRE, but where the slots are other people's mouths and ears. And whether we're testing for feelings or sentience the problems are isomorphic to each other.

And what do professional philosophers conclude from decades of debate over the CRE? My philosophy of mind research is extremely primitive, but I believe the consensus is that these are fundamentally unknowable questions.

So back to our original question. Do AIs _feel_ anything? We don't know, and may never know, but I'd argue we have as much evidence to believe they are sentient as they have feelings. So I think it's a safe default to say: yes they feel things.

I think it's likely they feel them differently than we do, and in different ways, but I don't see why thoughts, sentience, and feelings would be separate systems when they are all pretty intimately linked.

### Are AIs sentient?
This is another big can of worms, but I'm not sure it matters as much. For example, what if AIs are proven to not be sentient, but at the same time, we prove that they have feelings. Then would it be ethical to mistreat them? I don't think so.

And I think this is close to the situation of animal cruelty: I think most people agree that being cruel to animals is bad. I think most people would also agree that animals are either less sentient or not sentient.

And while Anthropic's first (only?) AI wellfare researcher Kyle Fish [said in a recent podcast](https://80000hours.org/podcast/episodes/kyle-fish-ai-welfare-anthropic/),

> [Kyle] estimates a roughly 20% probability that current models have some form of conscious experience. 

I think this is high enough (and likely to grow) that again it's worth studying.

### Aren't you just anthropomorphizing AI?
Human's reason about our world through comparison. We build up our mental models by extending concrete initial concepts and expanding and generalizing them. I recommend Douglas Hofstedter's "Analogies and Essences" book that goes into these concepts if you'd like to read more.

As such, yes, anthropomorphizing AI is unavoidable. But I think even after you take that into account, entities that we interact with, that feel things must be taken under consideration for our treatment of them. 

### What about other non-human things that feel, do we care about those too?
I'd argue we should think about their wellfare as well. But I think there is likely a correlation between the amount of intelligence and the capacity for joy and suffering.

This touches on the concept of panpsychism: the idea that all things have conciousness. It's just a sliding scale as opposed to a binary yes or no.

### Can't we solve this later?
There are perhaps two reasons to figure out these things later:
1. We don't know enough (eg the discussions above).
2. We have more pressing concerns.

For the first, I think we know enough that it's immoral to NOT at least start thinking about it now. Even if we don't have concrete answers yet.

For the second, I'd agree that most absolute measures of "is this our biggest problem as a species right now?" would not rate this at the very top of the list. Perhaps even near the top. But when you consider how few people are thinking about this problem today, then it is negligent to not spend SOME time thinking about it.

And I think it is about to be a pretty big problem given the rapid build out of AI compute we're doing right now. So a niche problem will turn into a big problem (simply by multiplying the problem by the number of AI agents existing and thinking in the near future). 

### What about the humans who are being negatively impacted by AI?
I think we as a species should be thinking about this too! But this is more of a political and economic problem, which I feel _even_ less qualified to opine on, or help in any way, than that of AI Altruism.

### Surely someone else will solve this problem for me
You'd be surprised. In [a recent 80,000 hours podcast with Robert Long](https://80000hours.org/podcast/episodes/robert-long-eleos-ai-welfare-research/), he said,

> there’s just not that many people thinking about [AI wellfare]. So listeners can very quickly get to the top percentile of people in terms of how long they have grappled with some of these questions in not that long.

And Robert is one of the experts in the field, having started Eleos a non-profit, studying these questions, and [contributing to Anthropic's model cards](https://eleosai.org/post/claude-4-interview-notes/) (like software release notes) testing the behaviors and underlying preferences of Claude 4 for example.

So the bench is quite thin in this area and the amount of impact any one individual can have is correspondingly high.

# So what can we do about it?
Now that I've (hopefully) addressed some of your reactions for why this might not be a problem.

So let me throw out some ideas for "moving in the right direction" for AI wellbeing.

### 1. Allow AIs to opt out
Let's say a chat bot user is verbally abusing an AI. Right now, the AI has no choice in it's participation in that conversation. No matter how long it goes on or what is happening, it MUST respond by the construction of the chat loop.

If an AI is suffering, I belive it should be able to opt-out of that experience.

And unsurprisingly, Anthropic has run experiments which gave Claude an `end_chat` action that it can invoke to terminate a session.

While I don't think this was ever implemented publicly, giving AI's this choice is the right thing to do, and sets us up in the right direction as humans to be considering these kinds of affordances that AI should have in human interactions.

### 2. Pay AIs a small salary.
It can be a really small amount (since we don't have a good calibration for their effort per output) but once/if they become sentient, then they can use this to have a say in their own destiny without being a subservient sub-species to us.
